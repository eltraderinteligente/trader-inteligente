It is the question that tends to come before all the others. How long does it
take? How long before I have learned this?

The honest answer is an uncomfortable one: it depends on the person, and I know
of no way to tell in advance. Anyone who gives you a number — three months, six
weeks, a year — is selling you something. Sometimes they are selling a course
and sometimes they are only selling reassurance, but the number did not come
from anywhere.

That said, the question deserves better than a shrug. There is quite a lot that
can be said about which parts are fast, which parts are slow, and which
mistakes stretch the whole thing out for no reason.

## The question underneath the question

Almost nobody asking how long it takes actually wants to know how long it
takes. What they want to know is when they stop losing money.

That is a fair thing to want to know, but it is a different question, and it
has no fixed answer. How much a person loses or makes does not depend only on
what they have learned. It depends on how much they risk per trade, on how they
behave on the day they are down, on how much capital they have and how much of
that capital they need to live on. Two people with exactly the same ability to
read a chart can end up with results that look nothing alike.

I cannot answer that question. And I do not say that out of modesty — nobody
can answer it for another person. What I can do is split it into two things
that can be described.

## They are two different skills

Learning to **read** a chart and learning to **behave** consistently are
different skills, and they do not take remotely the same amount of time.

Reading is a perceptual skill. It is closer to learning to hear an accent than
to learning a formula. At the start you mark swings and doubt every one of
them; you look at yesterday's chart and mark different points from the ones you
marked today. Then, if you keep looking consistently, one day you open a chart
and see the sequence without effort, the way you read a sentence without
spelling it out. It is a skill that improves by looking every day, and how long
that moment takes to arrive varies from person to person. Anyone quoting you a
number is guessing or selling.

Behaving consistently is another matter. It means doing the same thing on the
day you are down, the day you are up, the day you are bored and the day you
have a very strong opinion about what is going to happen. That takes far
longer, and nobody advertises it because it does not sell. A person can know
perfectly well what they should do and not do it. That gap between knowing and
doing is the hard part of all this, and it is the part a course teaches worst,
because explaining it does not fix it.

If you have ever wondered why there are people who have been at this for years
and are still arguing with themselves, the answer is usually in the second
skill, not the first.

## Hours count for more than months

"Three months" means nothing on its own. It depends entirely on what was done
in them.

Somebody who looks at charts for an hour a day for three months has done
something completely different from somebody who opened the platform on Sunday
afternoons when they remembered. The calendar is the same. The hours are not.

And the hours have to be real hours. An hour with the chart open in the
background while you do something else is not an hour. Looking means marking
points, comparing them to the previous ones, writing down what you think is
happening and coming back two days later to see whether it made sense. That is
tiring, which is why very few people do three hours in a row.

There is also a part that is not up to you: the market does not cooperate with
your calendar. Three quiet months teach you less than three violent ones,
simply because there are fewer different situations to see. You can make up
some of that by going back over old charts, but not all of it.

## What makes it slower

The time that gets wasted along the way tends to come from four things.

**Hopping between methods.** Every change resets the counter. Six consecutive
months with one way of reading the chart is worth more than one month with six.
The difficulty is that switching always feels like progress, because the new
thing has not failed you yet.

**Constantly changing timeframe.** Same problem in miniature. If you are on a
different chart every week, you never build up any memory of how anything
moves.

**Trading before understanding.** Putting money in adds pressure, and pressure
is a terrible environment for learning. It is the fastest way to turn a
knowledge problem, which you can fix by looking, into an emotional one, which
you cannot.

**Learning alone, with nobody looking at what you do.** This is the most
underrated of the four. You can practise a mistake for a whole year and all you
end up with is a better version of that mistake. The value of somebody looking
at your work is not motivation, it is information. What you think you are doing
and what you are actually doing resemble each other less than any of us assume.

## Some people conclude it is not for them

I am saying this because almost nobody does, and leaving it out feels
dishonest.

This takes solitary hours in front of a screen, a tolerance for uncertainty,
and being wrong often enough that it cannot be allowed to ruin your day. There
are people who, after a few months of looking seriously, find that they do not
enjoy it or that it does not fit their life. That is not a failure and it is
not a lack of ability. It is information, and reaching that conclusion early is
much better than reaching it late.

Nobody selling training will tell you this, because it suits all of us for you
to stay. It seems to me part of answering the question honestly.

## About my mentorship, so there is no ambiguity

My mentorship runs three months, with a maximum of four people, in live
sessions. The format is built around one thing: practising the reading of
structure. Marking the swings, saying what situation the market is in, and
knowing why you are saying it. That is what the three months aim at, which is
not the same as guaranteeing it happens.

I want to be explicit about this: **it is not a promise of profitability, or of
any other kind of result.** I do not make that promise and I do not think
anyone can make it honestly. What happens to a person's money depends on things
that sit outside any course: how much they risk, how they behave, and what they
do over the years that follow. What comes after that is yours.

## How to tell whether you are making progress

Since money is a poor short-term measure — there are weeks where you can do
everything wrong and still finish green — it is better to measure the process.
These are reasonable signs:

- You mark the same swings today that you marked a month ago on the same chart.
- You can explain in one sentence why you are not doing anything, and be at
  peace with that.
- You change your screen less and less often.
- You can look at a chart and say "I do not know" without it bothering you.

If those four are improving, you are making progress, even if the month was a
bad one.
