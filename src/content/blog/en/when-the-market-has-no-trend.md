Almost everything taught about charts is built around trends. The examples you
see are trends. The good-looking screenshots are trends. Then you open your own
chart on an ordinary Tuesday and price has spent six hours going back and forth
between the same two places, getting nowhere.

That Tuesday is not the exception. That Tuesday is the normal state.

## A range is the default

A trend is a situation of imbalance: there are people willing to pay more and
more, or to sell cheaper and cheaper, and price travels. It happens, and when
it does it is very visible, precisely because it stands out against everything
else.

The rest of the time price does the other thing: it moves between two areas and
neither gives way. In the first post we called that a range, the third of the
three possible situations. I do not have numbers to give you about how much
time a market spends in each state, and I am wary of anyone who offers them too
confidently. What I can tell you, after enough years of looking, is that if you
start counting sessions you will find a lot more of the second kind than the
first.

That has an uncomfortable consequence. A method that only works in a trend is
not a method that wins sometimes and sits still the rest of the time. It is a
method that spends most of its life losing slowly: entries that go nowhere,
exits by a hair, costs, and above all the wear of being wrong without
understanding why, because the method "is the same one as always".

It is an uncomfortable place to be, and changing the method almost never gets
you out of it. What is missing is the question that comes before it: are we in
a situation where this method has anything to do?

## Recognising it in time, not afterwards

The expensive way to find out you were in a range is to look at the chart once
it has already cut you to pieces. It is perfectly obvious there. It is always
perfectly obvious afterwards.

The cheap way is structure, and it warns you earlier.

Remember how an uptrend read: each high above the last and each low above the
last, both at once. A range starts showing when that chain stops making
progress. Specifically:

**A high that finishes level with the previous one.** Not below it, which would
be something else. Level. Price reached where it reached last time and could
not get through.

**A low that does not progress either.** The pullback comes back to roughly the
same place it left from last time.

Two of those in a row is enough to change the label. You do not need to wait
for a third or a fourth. In a healthy trend the points advance; when they stop
advancing, what you are looking at is no longer a trend, even if it does not
look like a box yet.

The most honest signal of all is a feeling, and it is worth listening to: when
marking the points starts to take effort, when you have to squint to decide
whether that high is above the last one or not, the answer is usually that they
are level. And that is a range.

## The most useful thing is usually nothing

This is the part that is hardest to accept, and I will say it straight: in a
range, the best thing you can often do with the chart is close it.

I know exactly how that sounds. It sounds like an excuse, or like there is
nothing left to teach. But look at why it is genuinely difficult, because it is
not a problem of knowledge.

The cost of sitting out is invisible. It shows up nowhere. There is no line in
your notes saying "did nothing today and that was correct". All you feel is
that you spent three hours in front of a screen with nothing to show, and that
feeling is very close to having wasted your time.

The cost of trading badly does show up — but late. Each individual entry looks
reasonable in the moment. The bill does not appear until you put two weeks side
by side and add up. By then you no longer connect it to having traded a range.
You connect it to bad luck, or to the method failing.

So what we have is a visible, immediate cost for doing nothing, against a real
but delayed cost for doing something. It is not strange that most people choose
to do something. You will choose it too, plenty of times.

What helps is making the other side visible: write down the days you identified
a range and did not trade, with the label attached. Watching that list grow
gives the decision a body it did not have before. It is still not comfortable.
But it stops feeling like nothing.

## Ranges resolve, but they do not tell you which way

Every range ends. Sooner or later price leaves the box and there is a trend
again. That much is certain.

What is not contained in the range is which side it leaves from.

This is worth stating plainly because there is a lot of imagination in this
part. Price touching the ceiling four times does not mean the fifth will break
through, nor that it will turn back again. A range lasting a long time does not
mean it is "due". A three-day range is not more ripe than a three-hour one. It
is just bigger.

You can have an opinion about which way it will go. I sometimes do. But that
opinion does not come from the range: it comes from somewhere else, usually
from what the higher timeframe is doing. Confusing "I have an idea" with "the
chart is telling me" is the same old mistake, and in a range it costs more,
because price passes the same place over and over and always looks like it is
about to make up its mind.

## And sometimes the range is not a range

This is where the multi-timeframe post comes back, and it is the part that
brings the most relief when it clicks.

A range on your detail chart is very often a clean pullback on your context
chart. Same thing as the coastline: those six hours of back and forth that look
like a shapeless mess on fifteen minutes are three candles of pause on four
hours, inside a move that is still perfectly orderly.

That changes what you do with the information, and it changes it in the usual
direction. If the context chart is still trending and the detail chart is in a
range, your job is not to solve the range. It is to wait for the pullback to
finish. The small range is not a problem to be traded. It is the inside of a
pause you had already identified.

The reverse works too, though it is less pleasant. If you go up to the context
timeframe and that is in a range as well, then there really is nothing to hold
on to. That is the day to close the chart without feeling bad about it.

## How to practise this week

1. Every day, using your fixed pair of timeframes, write one word for the
   context chart: up, down or range.
2. Write another for the detail chart. Two words a day, nothing more.
3. When the word is "range", note what made you decide it: which high failed to
   clear the last one, which low failed to progress.
4. At the end of the week, count how many days each word came up. Do not
   interpret anything yet. Just count.
5. Look separately at the days where context was trending and detail was in a
   range. Those are the ones that will teach you most.

Ten seconds a day, five days. What comes out of it is your own answer to how
much time the market spends without a trend, measured on your market and your
timeframe, which is worth more than any number I could hand you.
