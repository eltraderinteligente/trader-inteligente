A chart covered in coloured rectangles, some nested inside others, looks like a
prepared chart. It is not. It is a machine for agreeing with you: with twenty
zones on screen, every time price moves it touches one of them, and it is
impossible for it to do anything that is not "in a zone". A mark that is always
satisfied separates nothing from nothing.

So let us take it in order. Starting with what a zone is and what it is not.

## Why it is an area and not a line

The first thing that tends to happen when someone learns this is that they want
the exact price. The level. The number.

And you can draw it. You can put a horizontal line at a specific price and it
looks very clean on screen. The trouble is that the cleanliness is false. Price
did not turn around at a number. It turned around across a stretch. There was a
run, from here to here, where buyers ran out and sellers showed up. That
stretch has height.

When you draw a line instead of an area, you are claiming a precision the data
does not contain. Then the usual two things happen: either price turns three
points short of your line and you sit there thinking you just missed it, or it
runs four points past and you conclude the line "did not work". Neither of
those was the market's mistake. It was asking for an exactness the chart never
promised.

An area is more honest, because it admits what you actually know: something
happened around here, in this band, not at this point.

## What a zone actually is

A zone is the place price left in a hurry.

That is the whole definition. Look for somewhere on the chart where price was
relatively still and then shot off to one side, leaving large candles in a
single direction. The place it left from is the zone. If it left upward, that
band is a demand zone. If it left downward, it is a supply zone.

What you mark is the base of that move: the part where price was sitting before
it went, not the run it made afterwards.

The explanation that tends to help is this one. If price left somewhere very
quickly, it is reasonable to think people were left there unable to do what
they wanted to do. Orders that never got filled, because price did not stay
long enough. If price comes back through that same place, those people still
have the same intention.

I want to be clear about one thing: that is a way of explaining it, not
something you can verify on screen. You do not see the orders. You see that
price left quickly. The explanation helps you remember why you marked that band
and not another one, but it is not evidence of anything.

## The most important part of this post

A zone is a place to pay attention. It is not a place where something is going
to happen.

I am repeating it because it is the easiest part to skip. Marking a zone is not
a prediction. It does not mean price will
bounce there. It does not even mean price will reach it. It means exactly one
thing: if price arrives in this band, this is a moment to look carefully
instead of glancing.

What you decide after that is a separate step, and it depends on things the
zone does not contain: what the structure is doing, how much you are risking,
what you do when you are wrong. The zone tells you where to look. It does not
tell you what will happen or what to do.

If you ever catch yourself thinking "it has to turn here", the word that does
not belong is "has".

## Zones get used up

This one is easy to see and it changes how you work.

The first time price comes back to a zone, the zone is whole. Whatever was left
unresolved there is still unresolved.

The second time, it is not. Part of what was there got resolved on the first
visit. The zone still exists as a drawing on your chart, but there is less
inside it. The third time, less still.

So the third visit to a zone is not the same event as the first, even though it
looks identical on screen. This runs against instinct, because it feels like
the opposite: the more times a level holds, the stronger it seems. It works
closer to the other way round, and the moment a zone "finally gave way after
holding five times" is usually just that — there was nothing left in it.

The practical habit that comes out of this: once a zone has had its visit,
either delete it or mark it so you remember it is no longer whole. Deleting it
is the cleanest option. A clean chart is easier to read than a chart with a
memory.

## Why two zones beat twenty

Back to that chart covered in rectangles.

The problem with having many zones is not that they take up room. It is that
they stop meaning anything. A zone exists to separate the moments that deserve
attention from the moments that do not. If you mark everything, you have
separated nothing. You have drawn a map at one-to-one scale, which is as useful
as no map.

And the usual thing happens too. With twenty zones on screen there is always
one near price, so there is always an excuse available for whatever you already
wanted to do. It is the same self-deception from the last post in a different
costume: instead of hunting for the timeframe that agrees with you, you hunt
for the rectangle that does.

A test that works well is this: the move leaving the zone has to be obvious
without squinting. If you have to talk yourself into that move being strong, it
was not. On a normal chart that usually leaves two or three zones worth having,
not twenty.

## Where structure comes in

This is where it joins up with the earlier posts, and it is what turns a zone
from a drawing into something useful.

A zone is not worth the same everywhere. A demand zone in a market that has
been making higher highs and higher lows sits with what is already happening:
it is a band where the wider move could carry on. The same demand zone,
identical in shape, in a market making lower highs and lower lows, sits against
everything else on the chart.

Neither one guarantees anything. But the first is lined up with the structure,
and the second needs the market to change its mind before it makes sense.
Working almost entirely with the first is the sensible default, not because the
other is impossible, but because you do not need to argue with the chart in
order to have things to look at.

That is also why the order matters. Structure first, on the context timeframe.
Zones second, and only the ones running that way. Doing it backwards — finding
pretty zones and then checking what structure is up to — is how you end up with
twenty rectangles and no reading.

## How to practise this week

1. Open your context chart, from the pair you fixed last week.
2. Read the structure first and write it down. Do not draw anything yet.
3. Now find the two or three places on the chart where price obviously shot
   away, in the same direction as the structure.
4. Mark the base of each as an area, not a line. Let the rectangle cover the
   band where price sat before it left.
5. Write next to each zone whether price has already come back to it, and how
   many times.
6. If you ended up with more than four zones, remove the least obvious ones
   until you are down to three.

That last step is the real exercise. Choosing which to remove forces you to say
why one zone looked better than another, and that reason is the thing you are
actually learning.
