What you should have on screen is one of the earliest questions anyone starting
out asks. The easy answer is "whatever you like, it does not matter", and it is
a bad answer. It does matter: a badly built chart charges you a small toll
every day, in time spent moving things around and in doubts that should not
exist in the first place.

So this post is about that. How to set the chart up once, properly, and then
leave it alone.

## First of all: TradingView is not a broker

This is worth saying plainly because it gets confused a lot.

TradingView is a charting platform. It is for looking at price, marking it up,
saving layouts and studying. Trading with real money involves a separate
company, an account with its own paperwork, and a decision that **this post
does not cover**. I am not going to talk here about how to choose who you trade
with, because that is a separate subject, a longer one, and one with more
consequences.

They are two separate decisions, and mixing them is a fairly common way to end
up with money in the market before you have a reason to put it there. All we
are doing here is building somewhere to look.

A free account is enough to follow everything on this blog. There are paid
plans that add features, but nothing I teach in this series needs them. You do
not have to pay anything to learn to read structure.

## Making the account

Nothing to it. An email address you actually check, and that is that.

The one thing that does matter: make the account **before** you start
configuring anything. Your setup is tied to your account, and building the
whole chart while logged out and then losing it is a classic that happens to
a lot of people once.

## A clean chart

The default chart usually arrives with something already on it. Take it off.
All of it.

Then three decisions:

**Candles.** Not a line, not bars. A candle shows you the open, high, low and
close at a glance, and structure lives precisely in the highs and the lows. A
line chart only draws closes, which means it is hiding exactly the information
you need.

**Get rid of the noise.** The grid, extra labels, marks you cannot account for,
anything that moves or flashes. A simple rule to decide by: if you cannot say
what something on your screen is for, it should not be there.

**No indicators, for now.** I went through why I start this way in the previous
post and I will not repeat it. It is enough to say that you want to see the raw
material before the processed material.

## Colours you can look at for hours

This is not decoration. You are going to spend hours looking at that screen and
eye fatigue is real: when you get tired you stop seeing well, and you start
making worse decisions without noticing.

Dark background or light is a matter of taste and of the light in your room.
Something else matters more: that the contrast is sufficient without being
aggressive. Pure, heavily saturated colours on a black background tire the eye
quickly. The candle bodies need to be distinguishable at a glance, without you
having to concentrate.

A practical test: look at the chart for ten minutes straight, then look up at
something far away. If your eyes take a second to readjust, your contrast is
too high.

And one thing people forget: if you have trouble telling red from green, change
them. Candle colours are configurable, and there is no reason to fight the
default combination every single day.

## Save the layout

Once it looks the way you want, save it as a named layout.

The reason is not convenience. It is that if you do not save it, you rebuild it
every day, and rebuilding introduces variation: one scale today, another
tomorrow, a different zoom this week. When you are learning to read, having the
chart look the same every day matters far more than it sounds. You are training
your eye, and eyes are trained by repetition.

Having a second layout for a different timeframe is reasonable. Having twelve
is not, and it is usually the same old restlessness dressed up as organisation.

## A short watchlist

The temptation is to add everything you can think of. Resist it.

The scarce resource here is not space on the list, it is your attention.
Following forty instruments means glancing at forty and knowing none of them.
And reading structure depends quite a lot on memory: knowing what that
instrument has been doing for weeks, where it stalled last time, how it tends
to move. That memory does not survive forty names.

Five is plenty. Choose them on two criteria: that they move enough for there to
be something to read, and that they are active during the hours when you can
actually look. That second criterion brings us to the last point.

## The timezone

This is the setting most people have wrong and almost nobody catches, because
the chart still looks perfectly normal when it is wrong.

Your chart cuts candles according to a clock. A daily candle starts and ends at
a particular moment of the day, and that moment depends on the timezone you
have configured. If the timezone does not correspond to the session of the
market you are watching, your daily candles are cut somewhere else, and
therefore **your highs and lows are different points**. Not similar ones:
different ones. Every bit of structure you mark changes.

So decide one thing and be consistent: either use the timezone of the market
you follow, or use your own while knowing exactly how it relates to the
session. What you cannot do is not know which one you have set.

If you come to the mentorship sessions, bear in mind that we run them on US
Eastern Time. Having your chart lined up with that saves a good deal of
confusion when somebody says "look at what happened at ten".

## Do it once, then leave it alone

All of this is an afternoon's work. It is worth doing calmly and then not
touching again.

I say that because changing your screen is one of the most effective ways to
feel like you are working without working. Whole afternoons disappear into
colour schemes and reordered watchlists, and by the end of the day you have not
looked at a single chart. If something genuinely bothers you, change it — but
change one thing and leave it for a few weeks before touching anything else.

With the chart ready, the next thing is to start reading it. If you have just
arrived at this series, the place to begin is the first post:
[What is market structure?](/en/blog/what-is-market-structure). That is where
the exercise everything else builds on lives, and now you have a screen to do
it on properly.
