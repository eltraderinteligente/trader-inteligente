You will open the four-hour chart and see an obvious uptrend, then drop down to
the five-minute chart and see an equally obvious downtrend. Then you will sit
there trying to work out which of the two charts is lying to you.

Neither. The problem is the question.

## The same market, two different answers

In the earlier posts we settled on structure being the sequence of highs and
lows, and on price only ever being in one of three situations once you have
that sequence in front of you. All of that still holds. What I left out is that
the answer changes depending on which chart you have open, and that both
answers can be right at the same time.

Think about the Florida coastline seen from a plane. It is a smooth line, one
long curve and not much else. Now go down to the beach and walk that same
coastline: there are rocks, inlets, a fallen trunk, a bend running the opposite
way to the big curve. Nobody would say the view from the plane was false. It is
the same coastline at a different resolution.

A chart is exactly that. The four-hour and the five-minute are not two markets.
They are the same movement seen from two heights. What the four-hour shows as a
three-candle pullback inside a move up, the five-minute shows as a complete
downtrend, with its lower highs and lower lows, perfectly readable. Both
readings are true. They just describe things of different sizes.

Once that is clear, the question of which chart is right falls away on its own
and a better one is left: which of these two sets the context, and which one is
for detail?

## Pick a pair and stay on it

The simple way to do it is this. Two charts, with two fixed and separate jobs.

**The context chart** is in charge. It is where you read the structure that
matters: uptrend, downtrend or range. That chart decides which direction makes
sense for anything you do afterwards. It does not change during a session.

**The detail chart** is where you look closely. It shows the movement at higher
resolution, it makes the points easier to place, and it shows the moment price
actually does something. It is not in charge. If the detail chart says one
thing and the context chart says another, the context chart still owns the
scenario.

What matters is not which pair you pick. It is that it is always the same one.
A fixed pair makes your reading comparable from one day to the next. If you
look at daily and one hour on Monday, four hours and fifteen minutes on
Tuesday, and something else on Wednesday, you do not have a method. You have
three, and none of them has enough history for you to learn anything from it.

## The most common self-deception in this part of the craft

I am going to name it plainly, because it is a common mistake and an expensive
one.

Hopping between timeframes until you find one that agrees with you.

It works like this. You have an idea. You open the four-hour and the idea does
not hold up. You drop to one hour: still no. You drop to fifteen minutes and
there it is, a higher low, and now you have your justification. You never tell
yourself "I am going to go find a chart that gives me permission". You tell
yourself "I am going to refine the read". Same thing in better clothes.

The tell is simple, and it works even on yourself: if you are changing
timeframe **after** having the idea, you are not analysing. If the pair was
decided before you looked, you cannot fall into this at all, because there is
nothing left to go shopping for.

One way to get rid of the problem is to write the pair down before opening the
platform. Not through discipline. By removing the temptation.

## How far apart they should sit

There is no correct number, but there is a band in which two charts become
useful instead of redundant. Think of it as a ratio rather than a prescription.

If the two timeframes sit too close together — fifteen minutes and thirty, say
— the second chart tells you almost nothing new. You are looking at the same
movement with a couple more candles in it. You are spending half your screen to
repeat yourself.

If they sit too far apart — daily and one minute — you lose the thread between
them. A pullback on the daily contains so many one-minute candles that there is
no way to relate the two by eye. By the time the top chart moves, hours have
gone by on the bottom one.

As a rule of thumb, a separation of somewhere between four and six times tends
to keep the two charts connected and still distinct. Four hours with one hour.
One hour with fifteen minutes. Daily with four hours. Treat that as a starting
point rather than a rule of the market: if a slightly wider gap is more
comfortable to read, the ratio is the point, not the specific numbers.

## Why the third chart almost never helps

The urge to add a third one is strong. More information ought to be better
information.

It almost never is. With two charts, when they disagree, you have a clear
hierarchy: the context chart wins and that is the end of it. With three, when
the top says one thing, the middle another and the bottom a third, you do not
have more information. You have a three-way tie, and what breaks it is your
mood that morning.

The third chart also reopens the door to the problem above. The more timeframes
you have on screen, the easier it is for one of them to agree with you.

If you feel short of context, the fix is usually not to add a chart but to move
the whole pair up: go from one hour and fifteen minutes to four hours and one
hour. You still have two. You are just looking further out.

## How to practise this week

You do not need to trade anything for this one either.

1. Choose a pair of timeframes and write it on paper before you open anything.
   Four hours and one hour works fine.
2. Open the context chart and read the structure out loud, the way we did in
   the first post. Write down the situation: up, down or range.
3. Without changing that conclusion, drop to the detail chart and find the
   stretch that matches the last pullback on the bigger chart.
4. Describe that too. You will see that the pullback has a full structure of
   its own on the inside, often running against the one above.
5. Repeat for five days in a row with the same pair. No swapping, however much
   it itches.

What usually happens is that around day three it starts to feel comfortable,
and the detail chart stops looking like a contradicting opinion and becomes the
inside of something you already knew was there.
