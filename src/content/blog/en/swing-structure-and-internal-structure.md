As soon as you learn to mark swings, an odd effect shows up: you start seeing
reversals everywhere. You close a position convinced the trend has turned, and
a while later price is still going the same way as before, without you. The
explanation is usually fairly simple. You are reading two different structures
as if they were one.

This post is about those two structures, and about why it pays to say out loud
which of the two you are looking at.

## The skeleton and what happens inside it

**Swing structure is the skeleton: the big turning points that define the
trend.** They are the points that survive when you step back from the chart.
Print the screen out and look at it from across the room, and what you can
still see are the swings.

**Internal structure is the sequence of highs and lows that happens between two
of those points.** Price does not travel in a straight line from one swing to
the next. It goes up a bit, pulls back, goes up more, pulls back again. That
zigzag has highs and lows of its own, and they can be read with the same three
situations as always: up, down, or range.

So on the same chart, at the same moment, there are two descriptions running.
One describes the whole leg. The other describes what is happening inside the
leg. Both are true. They are talking about things of different sizes.

Notice this is not the same as looking at two timeframes. You are still on the
same chart, with the same candles. The only thing that changes is the size of
move you are willing to count as a point.

## Read only the small one and you get whipsawed

This is a common mistake, and an expensive one.

If you mark every high and low that appears, minor ones included, your reading
changes several times a day. A normal pullback inside an advance produces,
seen up close, a lower high and a lower low. That is to say: it produces a
miniature downtrend. If you are not separating the sizes, what you see is "the
trend has reversed", and you act on it. Then price finishes the pullback,
resumes the advance, and you are outside wondering what happened.

Repeated thirty times, this costs you twice. The obvious cost is the bad
decisions. The less obvious one is that it teaches you to distrust the method.
If structure tells you something different every two hours, you end up
concluding that structure does not work. It was not structure. You were reading
the fine print of one paragraph and drawing conclusions about the book.

## Read only the big one and you are late

The opposite mistake exists too, and it is just as real, though it makes less
noise.

If you only look at the big swings, your reading is stable but slow. A swing is
confirmed once price has already travelled a long way, so anything you decide
on the skeleton alone gets decided well after the move began. For some ways of
trading that is perfectly fine, and even desirable. For others it means the
interesting part already happened.

There is also this: when you only see the skeleton, you have no way of noticing
that something is changing before it has fully changed. Internal structure is
the first thing to deteriorate. It is not a signal and it is not a prediction,
but it is information, and throwing it away entirely is throwing information
away.

## Name which one you are talking about

Out of all this comes a practical habit that is very easy to adopt, and it is
purely a matter of vocabulary.

Before you say "the trend", say which one. "Swing structure is up." "Internal
structure is down." Two sentences, not one. They cost you an extra second and
they remove almost all of the confusion.

It sounds like pointless formality until you try it for a week. What happens is
that the incomplete sentence — "the trend is up" — lets you blend the two
readings without noticing. The complete sentence forces you to know which one
you are using, and if you cannot complete it, that is because you did not have
it clear. Which is useful information on its own.

The same goes for levels. "It broke the previous low" says nothing until you
specify whether that was a swing low or an internal low. Those are events of
very different sizes.

## The two disagreeing is normal

Here is something worth stating plainly, because it gets misread often: **the
two structures disagreeing is not a signal of anything.**

It is the ordinary state of affairs. Internal structure turns down every time
there is a pullback inside an advance, and pullbacks are half of what price
does. If you counted the days, you would find the two readings agree less of
the time than you would expect.

The disagreement describes a situation. It does not announce an outcome. It
says: inside this up-leg, price is currently correcting. That is all it says.
It does not say how long the correction will last, or whether it will end where
the previous ones ended, or whether this time the big leg will fail. When
somebody turns that disagreement into a concrete expectation, they are adding
something structure does not contain.

## An up-leg with a down-move inside it

Let us go through one slowly, because this is easier to follow in words than
people expect.

Take an up-leg of the swing structure: it runs from a swing low to a swing
high, and in between price rose over several days. That leg, as a unit, is a
single move upward. On the skeleton it is two points and a line between them.

Now zoom in and look at what price actually did inside that line. It rose, made
a peak and pulled back. It rose again, took out the previous peak, and pulled
back once more, this time a little deeper. So far the internal structure is
also up: the internal peaks are rising and the internal troughs are rising.

But at some point inside the leg this happens: price makes an internal peak
that finishes below the previous internal peak, and then falls below the
previous internal trough. Read on the internal structure, that is a downtrend,
properly formed. Three or four frightening candles if you are watching closely.

And yet on the skeleton, absolutely nothing happened. The swing low that opened
the leg is still intact, far below. Price corrected inside the leg, found
buyers, and carried on toward the swing high that would close it. Swing
structure never changed state. Internal structure changed twice.

Both descriptions were correct the whole time. They were answering different
questions.

## How to practise this week

1. Open a clean chart and mark the big swings first, using the rule you wrote
   down last week. Only those. You should end up with few points.
2. Pick one leg, just one, between two of those swings.
3. Inside that leg, and without changing timeframe, now mark the small
   sequence: the internal highs and lows. Use a different colour.
4. Write two sentences beside the chart: "swing structure in this leg is ___"
   and "internal structure in this leg did ___".
5. Repeat with three or four more legs, and note how many times internal
   structure went against the leg without the big leg breaking.

That last number is the one that will help you most. Seeing that the
contradiction is frequent and almost always inconsequential takes a lot of the
drama out of pullbacks.

Of course, saying "the big leg did not break" assumes we know exactly what
breaking means. That is the next post: how to know when a trend is really
broken, and why confusing a pullback with a break is probably the most
expensive mistake there is in reading structure.
