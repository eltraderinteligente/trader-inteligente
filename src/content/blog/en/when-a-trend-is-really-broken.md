The phrase "the trend broke" is almost always used to mean something else. What
it usually means is "this is making me uncomfortable". Those are two different
sentences and they sound the same, so there is no way to go back afterwards and
check whether the reading was right. If your criterion is a feeling, there is
nothing to check.

This post is about turning that phrase into something you can check.

## A definition, not a feeling

Go back to what an uptrend is: higher highs and higher lows, both at once. That
is the description. So the question "is it broken?" has a mechanical answer. It
is broken when the description stopped being true.

And for it to stop being true, two things have to happen, **in this order**:

1. Price tries to go up and fails to take out the previous high. It falls short
   and turns back down. At that point there are no more higher highs.
2. Then price falls below the highest low it left before that attempt. At that
   point there are no more higher lows.

Once both have happened, the sentence "this is an uptrend" has become false. It
is not a matter of opinion. Anybody looking at the same chart with the same
definition reaches the same conclusion.

In a downtrend it is exactly the reverse: price fails to make a lower low, and
then takes out the previous lower high.

## The order is half the point

This is an easy part to skip, so I am giving it its own section.

Price falling below a previous low, on its own, does not break anything. If
before that drop price had made a higher high, what you are looking at is a
deep pullback inside a trend that is still alive by its own definition. It is
uncomfortable, but uncomfortable and broken are not the same thing.

And the other way round: price falling short on an attempt at a new high does
not break anything on its own either. A high that fails to clear the previous
one is a sign that the push is fading, and nothing more. As long as the
previous low is untouched, the structure is still what it was.

You need both events, in that order. First the failed attempt, then the loss of
the floor. Get into the habit of demanding the full sequence and you stop
declaring charts broken three times a week.

## Pullback and break

Confusing those two is one of the most expensive mistakes there is in reading
structure. More expensive than marking a swing badly, more expensive than being
on the wrong timeframe.

A pullback is a move against the structure that happens **inside** it, without
changing the description. Price comes down, sometimes a long way, but leaves
the previous low untouched and then resumes.

A break is a move against the structure that **changes** the description. Both
events happened and the old sentence no longer applies.

Watched live, while price is falling, the two look identical. That is the whole
difficulty. There is nothing in the falling candle that tells you which one it
is. The only thing that distinguishes them is where it stops, and you only know
that afterwards. What you can do is have marked, in advance, the exact level at
which a pullback becomes a break. That level exists, it is a specific price,
and it is on the chart before anything happens.

## What a break tells you, and what it does not

This is where a lot of people get lost. Once you finally learn to identify
breaks properly, it is very easy to start treating them as the beginning of
something. They are not.

**A break of structure tells you the old description no longer applies. That is
all it tells you.**

It does not tell you a trend has started in the other direction. For there to
be a downtrend you would need lower highs and lower lows, and so far there are
none of either: there is one high that fell short and one low that was lost.
That is not a downtrend. It is the absence of an uptrend, which is a different
thing.

What very often follows a break is a range. The market stops going up, does not
start going down, and spends its time turning over between two areas while it
makes up its mind. If your way of reading only has two boxes — up and down —
you have nowhere to put that situation, and you will force it into the wrong
box. That is why I made such a point in the first post of there being three
situations, not two.

The honest thing to say after a break is: "the uptrend is over and I do not yet
know what this is now." It is an unexciting sentence and it is almost always
the correct one.

## "Broken" on the 15-minute and "broken" on the daily

These are two different sentences about two different things, and they sound
the same.

A break on the 15-minute chart means the sequence of highs and lows over the
last few hours stopped being an uptrend. That is a real fact and it is a small
fact. On the daily chart, that same move is probably one red candle inside a
normal pullback, and the daily structure did not notice.

Neither reading is truer than the other. They are statements about different
horizons. The problem shows up when somebody reads a break on the 15-minute and
draws a daily-sized conclusion from it, or when somebody reads the daily
structure and dismisses the 15-minute entirely.

Again, the fix is vocabulary. Do not say "the trend broke". Say "swing
structure broke on the hourly chart". The longer sentence forces you to be
precise, and precision is the only thing that lets you go back later and check
whether you were right.

## You will call it wrong sometimes

I have to say this, because it is true and because it almost never gets said.

Even with a clear definition, a written rule and the timeframe stated, you will
be wrong. You will see the full break sequence and price will turn back up and
carry on climbing as if nothing had happened. It is called a false break and it
is part of the landscape. It does not happen because you read it badly. It
happens because the market is under no obligation to continue what it started.

The practical consequence of accepting that is one thing, and it is the
important part of this post: **the level that would prove your reading wrong
has to be identified before you act, not after.** Before you do anything with
the information, look at the chart and answer this: what price, if the market
reaches it, means that what I just read was false? That price always exists and
it is usually obvious.

Looking for it afterwards does not work, and not because of a lack of
discipline. It is that afterwards you have an opinion involved, and with an
opinion involved the level moves on its own.

## How to practise this week

1. Open a clean chart, on a wide timeframe, with the swings marked according to
   your rule.
2. Go back and find the last three places where the trend broke by the full
   definition: failed attempt first, loss of the previous point second. Mark
   them.
3. Now find three places where price fell hard but the sequence was **not**
   completed. Mark those in a different colour. Notice how many of them would
   have looked like breaks to you live.
4. For each of the three real breaks, cover the chart from that point onward
   and write down what you expected to happen next. Then uncover it.
5. Count how many were followed by a trend in the other direction and how many
   were followed by a range.

That last count is usually the most useful exercise in this whole series. It
changes what you expect from a break, and expecting the right thing is worth
more than being right.
