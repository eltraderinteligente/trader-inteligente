Mark up a chart on a Monday, open it again on Thursday without a rule, and you
will mark different points. Same chart, same candles, not one new candle in
the area you were looking at. And yet on Monday you saw an uptrend and on
Thursday you see a top.

The problem is not the market. The problem is that there is no rule. You mark
whatever looks important, and what looks important depends quite a lot on what
happens to suit you at the time.

The last post ended exactly there: structure is only useful if you mark the
same points every day. This post is about how to actually do that.

## A real turn and just any spike

**A swing is a point where price genuinely turned around.** The word doing the
work in that sentence is "genuinely", and that is the word we have to define.

On any chart there are hundreds of candles whose high sits above the high of
the candle before it and the candle after it. Technically all of them are
points where price went up and then came down. If you mark all of them, the
chain of highs and lows becomes useless: you end up with forty points on one
screen and no sequence you can read. It is like describing the shape of a hill
by counting the stones.

A swing is a spike that had consequences. After it, price did not make a
gesture — it went somewhere. That is the whole filter. It does not matter how
long the candle was, or whether it had a big wick, or whether it felt dramatic
to you at the time. What matters is what happened afterwards.

## Why you need a rule, not a judgement

You might think the eye learns this with practice and that is that. What
usually happens is closer to the opposite.

The eye does learn, but it also learns to please you. When you are long, minor
lows start to look like important lows, because if they count as lows then the
uptrend is still alive and you do not have to do anything uncomfortable. When
you are short the same thing happens in reverse. This is not bad faith or a
lack of discipline. It is that you are looking at an ambiguous chart with money
on the table, and in that situation everybody sees what suits them.

A rule takes the decision out of your hands. There stops being a question —
"does this one count?" — and there starts being a check: it qualifies or it
does not. And the valuable thing about a rule is not that it is the best
possible rule. It is that it is the same rule on Monday and on Thursday.

## The rule: make price move away

A simple version, good enough to start with, has two conditions.

**First: the point has to be the extreme of its neighbourhood.** For a swing
high, the high of that candle sits above the highs of the two or three candles
before it and the two or three after it. Pick a number and leave it alone.
Whether it is two or three hardly matters. What does matter is that you do not
use two today and four tomorrow because four gives you the picture you wanted.

**Second: price then has to move away.** An extreme surrounded by slightly
lower candles is not a turn yet. What makes it a turn is that from there price
travels a distance comparable to the legs that chart has been making. I am not
giving you a number, because the number depends on the instrument and on the
moment: what counts as a serious move on one chart is an ordinary candle on
another. You make that comparison against the chart in front of you.

The second condition does most of the work, and it is also the annoying one,
because it means waiting. A swing is confirmed late by definition. At the
moment the turn happens you do not know it is a turn; you know once price has
already left. That is not a flaw in the method, it is the nature of the thing.
Any rule that confirms a swing instantly is confirming it with information that
does not exist yet.

## A 5-minute swing does not exist on the daily

This confuses a lot of people at the start, and it is simpler than it looks.

A swing is not a property of the market. It is a property of the chart you are
looking at. On the 5-minute chart, a turn that lasted forty minutes and covered
a decent leg is a perfectly valid swing: it passes the rule, you can see it,
you mark it. On the daily chart that same move sits inside a single candle. It
is not that it is a small swing on the daily. It is that on the daily it does
not exist.

That is why two people can look at the same instrument in the same minute and
describe different structures without either of them being wrong. One is on
5 minutes and the other is on the daily. They are describing different things
with the same words.

Which gives you a habit worth adopting right now: before you say anything about
structure, say which timeframe you are on. "The previous high" means nothing on
its own. "The previous high on the hourly" means something.

## No rule removes every judgement call

I want to be straight about this, because it sometimes gets sold the other way.

However precise you make the rule, there will be borderline cases. Points that
almost qualify. Moves that travelled slightly less than you expected. Areas
where there are two candidates for the high three candles apart and neither is
clearly the right one. You do not fix that by refining the rule further. If you
add conditions to cover every odd case, you end up with something so
complicated that you are back to deciding by eye, only now with extra steps.

The goal is not a perfect rule. It is a consistent one. Apply the same mediocre
rule every day and your reading of structure will be stable, which means you
can notice when you are wrong. Apply five excellent rules depending on the day
and you cannot learn anything, because you will never know which part failed.

One way to handle the doubtful ones is to mark them in a different colour and
move on. A doubtful point is information, not a problem. Very often the
area that looks unclear to you is the area where the market was not clear
either.

## How to practise this week

Again, you do not need to trade anything for this.

1. Write your rule down on paper, including the number of candles on each side.
   Written down, not held in your head. That is the step almost everybody
   skips.
2. Open a clean chart on a wide timeframe and mark the swings of the last two
   or three months using that rule and only that rule.
3. Close the chart. The next day, open the same chart without looking at your
   old marks and mark it again from scratch.
4. Compare the two versions. The points that do not match are a map of where
   your rule is ambiguous.
5. Adjust the rule once to close that ambiguity, and then leave it alone for a
   full month.

The day those two versions come out almost identical, you have a base you can
build something on. Before that, anything you learn on top is sitting on a
drawing that changes by itself.

In the next post we will look at something that shows up as soon as you start
marking properly: inside every leg of the big structure there is a smaller
structure, with its own highs and lows. They are called swing structure and
internal structure, and mixing them up is one of the most common reasons
somebody believes the trend has reversed when nothing has happened.
